What is Business Intelligence (BI)?
• BI is defined as providing decision makers with valuable information and
knowledge by leveraging a variety of data sources as well as structured
and unstructured information (Sabherwal 2007, 2008).
• BI utilizes:
• Data (obtained from various sources external or internal to the
organization);
• Information which could be quantitative or qualitative.
• BI output is KNOWLEDGE.

What is Business Intelligence (BI)?
• BI has been used in two different ways:
• Product or the information and knowledge that are useful to
organizations for their business activities and decisions.
• Process, where an organization obtains, analyzes and distributes
such information or knowledge.
• BI Tools vs BI Solutions
• BI tools: used in BI solutions developed by BI vendors
• BI solutions: support the BI process deployed within organization.
• BI solutions utilize the BI tools acquired by the organization, and
draw upon the vast amounts of data from existing data warehouses
and transaction processing systems, as well as structured and
unstructured information from these and other sources to provide
information and knowledge that facilitate decision making.

# Engagement 1: BI Product, Process, Solution
and Tools
Case Study:
Tesco, a grocery chain in the UK, employs BI tools for effective data access
and analytics. It collects detailed data on purchases by using customer-
rewards cards, and then uses a BI solution to categorize customers and to
develop knowledge on how offers should be customized. The BI process
thus benefits from the BI solution, but it is broader in nature, and includes
Tesco's tracking redemption rates in detail. This application of BI to
generate useful information (about customer categories) and knowledge
(about potentially beneficial changes in internal processes) has enabled
Tesco to modify its business processes to obtain better response from
customers. Consequently, Tesco has been able to raise the redemption rate
for its direct-marketing initiatives to about 20%, which is much greater
than the industry norms of about 2%, and has thereby increased its sales.

Factors that drive BI
1. Exploding data volumes
The confluence of technological progress (improved data storage capabilities as well as the
tremendous increase in electronic connections through the Internet and intranet) and regulatory
changes has led to a dramatic increase in the data collected and stored by organization.
• Cheaper storage
• More electronic connections (Internet, intranet,…)
• Regulatory changes (the Sarbanes Oxley Act of 2002, which requires senior executives in
publicly traded firms to be actively involved in their firm’s information assets)
BI solutions provide managers the ability to more effectively utilize these larger data volumes.
--- PDF PAGE 18 ---
Factors that drive BI
2. Increasingly complicated decisions
With increasing competition from across industries and across countries, decision making in
organizations has become increasingly complicated, at least in terms of the variety of factors that
need to be considered.
• Multi-industry, global competition
• Decisions from structured and unstructured data
BI solutions provide managers the ability to make decisions that incorporate all the important factors
and are based on integration across the structured and unstructured sources of information.
--- PDF PAGE 19 ---
Factors that drive BI
3. Need for quick reflexes
The pace of change, or volatility, within each market domain has increased rapidly in the past
decade.
For example, market and environmental influences can result in overnight changes in an
organization.
Due to the acceleration in the pace at which the global economy operates, the time available for
organizations to respond to market and environmental changes has been decreasing.
This makes it critical that managers be able to quickly access actionable information, so that
decisions can be made and implemented before the window opportunity closes.
--- PDF PAGE 20 ---
Factors that drive BI
3. Need for quick reflexes
Three kinds of delays constrain such quick reflexes:
• Converting data from variety of sources;
• Integrating information across sources;
• Making the results available to the decision maker.
Effective BI solutions help address each of the three types of delays.
Decision must be made and implemented quickly – otherwise the window of opportunity closes.

BI in contemporary organizations
Four Contributions of BI:
1. Dissemination of user-friendly, real-time information
2. Creation of new knowledge based on the past
3. Responsive and anticipative when making decisions that are:
• Based more closely on all the latest information, and
• Incorporate predictions regarding the future
4. Improved planning for the future through:
• More effective use of information
• Use of past data for predictions about the future
• Development of knowledge based on information about the past
--- PDF PAGE 35 ---
BI in contemporary organizations
Three Benefits of BI to Organizational Success:
1. Improvement in operational performance
• Provide real-time information on how the organization is performing.
• Help managers to identify aspects that need improvement.
For example, BI tools helped an oil and gas company, which was facing cash
flow problems, to recognize that although it was delivering gas to customers
on time, it was sending the invoices a week too late. Identification of this
underlying problem led to the company taking measures to institute a new
rule requiring invoicing within a day of delivery (Howson, 2008).
2. Improvement in customer service
• Improve quality of customer service provided
• Identify problems and potential solutions quickly
• Reduce customer concerns and improve retention

BI in contemporary organizations
Three Benefits of BI to Organizational Success:
For example, mobile operators could use BI solutions to identify customers
that are likely to move to another carrier, by analyzing their airtime usage
patterns and other behaviours, and target these customers for efforts
intended to prevent them from defecting (Howson, 2008).
3. Identification of new opportunities
• Facilitate new insights through discover of unknown patterns
• Track innovative projects more effectively
For example, faced with increasing competition and rising consumer needs,
better understand the preferences and needs of its high-value customers (i.e.
the 27% customers who generated 80% of its income), and improve
customer service by targeting their specific needs (Rogalski, 2005).

2. Agile development

• Break the task into smaller chunks – make them work and build upon them

Dimensions and Measures
When you connect to a new data source, Tableau assigns each field in the
data source to either the Dimensions area or the Measures area of the Data
pane, depending on the type of data the field contains. You use these fields
to build viewsof your data.
DataField
Data fields are made from the columns in your data source. Each field is
automatically assigned a data type (such as integer, string,date), and a role:
Discrete Dimension or Continuous Measure (more common), or Continuous
Dimension or Discrete Measure(lesscommon).
• Dimensions contain qualitative values (such as names, dates, or
geographical data). You can use dimensions to categorize, segment,
and reveal the details in your data. Dimensions affect the level of detail
intheview.
• Measures contain numeric, quantitative values that you can measure.
Measures can be aggregated. When you drag a measure intothe view,
Tableau applies an aggregation to that measure (bydefault).
Blue versus green fields
Tableau representsdata differentlyinthe view depending on whether the field
is discrete (blue), or continuous (green). Continuous and discrete are
mathematical terms. Continuous means "forming an unbroken whole, without
interruption"; discrete means "individually separate anddistinct."
